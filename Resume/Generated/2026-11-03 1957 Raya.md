## Cover letter

To the Places engineering team at Raya,

Places is an app where the product is the quality of the experience: a curated guide has to feel considered on every screen, a Table Tonight reservation has to reflect availability that changed a moment ago, and a paid membership has to earn its renewal every year. The last large consumer flow I owned had the same shape. As Principal Software Engineer on the iOS checkout team at Walmart, I was responsible for the checkout flow in the flagship iOS app serving 5M+ daily active users and owned its shared architecture, which a 120+ engineer organization consumed across multiple apps. Checkout is where client state and server state disagree most expensively, so most of my time went to lifecycle and state problems: what the client may assume between a tap and a server response, how partial failures surface, and how a cart, a price, and a payment stay correct across backgrounding, retries, and concurrent edits from another device. I would like to bring that to the Places client team.

On Swift, UIKit, MVVM, and reactive frameworks: the Walmart platform was unidirectional, reactive design in Swift and Combine on UIKit, with MVVM-style patterns, modularized with Swift Package Manager. Before Combine existed I spent six years on RxSwift at WalmartLabs. I converted the ASDA UK app's architecture to reactive programming using RxSwift, then helped move the Sam's Club app to an RxSwift-based architecture and led the refactor of its checkout to a unidirectional, reactive design, migrating the Objective-C codebase to Swift along the way without interrupting service for roughly two million daily users. I have been building native iOS apps since 2010, with more than 20 shipped to the App Store across healthcare, aviation events, training, and consumer domains before and alongside the decade at Walmart.

On partnering with product, design, and data, and owning what ships after it ships: at Walmart I worked with product and design early to shape feature ideas and took them through architectural review before leading implementation. Every new checkout feature launched behind a feature flag, most as A/B experiments measuring customer and business impact, and I owned the analytics for those features, from instrumentation through dashboards to monitoring during rollout. I held a 99.9%+ crash-free session rate on the weekly production releases, used Firebase tooling to run customer-reported issues to ground, and collaborated with backend teams on GraphQL schema design and mobile-optimized APIs so the client and server agreed on the shape of the data before either side wrote code. I helped establish the iOS engineering standards for architecture, performance, security, and testing, mentored a dozen engineers, built the onboarding materials, and served as an interview bar-raiser. On the solo app I shipped this year, every feature started as a written spec, 238 of them, before implementation.

On AI coding tools: I use Claude Code and Codex daily, with GitHub Spec Kit for spec-first development and XcodeBuildMCP for build and simulator automation, and I treat the surrounding harness as part of the job. At CLEAR this year I worked with a six-engineer iOS team on bringing agentic tooling into their day-to-day workflow while maintaining and extending their Fastlane and GitHub Actions CI/CD configuration. At Walmart I assisted the platform team in evaluating Builder.io against the design library and trained 80+ iOS engineers on it through live sessions after engineering management adopted it as the standard for new view work.

The most direct evidence on both AI tools and maps is a demo I built this spring. Routy is a flight-history visualizer: add a flight, the backend lazy-fetches its real ADS-B trajectory from FlightAware's AeroAPI and caches it in Postgres, and the iOS app renders every flight you have taken as a ribbon on a Mapbox globe. I set the architecture and the acceptance bar and directed Claude Code agents through the build end to end, SwiftUI and TCA on the client, Kotlin and Ktor on the backend, deployed on Fly.io; the agents wrote the code, and my attention went to the client and the globe. The globe runs one GeoJSON source through glow, main, and selected line layers with data-driven expressions for recency, corridor frequency, and color, and filters cross-fade because they live in the opacity expression rather than in a layer filter. A TCA TestStore suite in Swift Testing was the bar the agents' client code had to clear. It is a demo with synthetic dates over real track geometry, not a shipped app, and I would say the same in an interview.

On your nice-to-have list: for McGraw-Hill's Sharpen study app I worked on the in-app subscription experience over StoreKit through RevenueCat, including the paywall and purchase and restore flows, free-trial and introductory-offer eligibility, Offerings fetched at runtime rather than hard-coded product IDs, and entitlement state synced to the backend after purchase with retry handling. For a physical-therapy prescription app I implemented auto-renewable subscriptions and took them through App Store review, and I gave a CocoaHeads talk on that implementation. At CLEAR I did new feature work in SwiftUI inside a long-lived UIKit app, and the fire-department app I shipped in June is SwiftUI with Swift 6 strict concurrency, structured as 16 Swift packages. On the backend side I built and operate that app's Supabase backend, built an AWS data lake and push notifications at McGraw-Hill, and spent the decade before iOS as a Java architect building the services mobile and web clients depend on.

Two gaps to be direct about. On Mapbox, my experience is directing and reviewing the Routy globe rather than writing against the SDK by hand; the map I wrote myself this year is the MapKit incident map in the fire-department app, with unit pins, radius overlays, a hydrant layer, and inline address search. My subscription work went through RevenueCat over StoreKit rather than directly against the StoreKit 2 APIs, and my pricing experiments were on checkout features, not paywalls, so the experiment discipline transfers but the paywall-specific analytics would be new.

The posting is a Senior role and my last title was Principal. Titles have never decided for me; building and owning member-facing features on a client team is the work I want. I work from Ohio and am set up for a fully remote role.

I would welcome a conversation about what the Places iOS app needs next and where client-server state sync sits on that list.

Doug Sjoquist
